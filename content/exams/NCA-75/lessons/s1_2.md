# The Products Around NCI (S1.2)

*Module S1.2 — Nutanix NCA 7.5 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

NCI is the foundation. This lesson is about the products that stand on it, and it is deliberately a recognition-level lesson: you are not expected to configure any of them. You are expected to read a short problem statement and know which *family* of product is the answer, the way you would know that a flat tyre is a tyre problem before you know which brand of tyre to buy.

## Why this layer exists

If NCI gives you compute, storage, and virtual machines, you could build everything else yourself — install a file server, a database, a container platform, a desktop broker. Many organisations do exactly that. The products in this lesson exist because building those things by hand is repetitive, and because doing it consistently across many clusters is harder than doing it once.

So the pattern to look for is always the same: **a job that everyone does the same way, wrapped into a product.** When a question describes a recurring operational chore, an adjacent product is usually the answer.

## Storage: the three shapes of data

NCI's own storage pool presents storage to virtual machines as disks. Real applications often want something more specific, and three products cover the three classic shapes:

- **File storage** — shared folders that many machines mount at once, reached over SMB or NFS. Think of a departmental share or a home directory.
- **Block storage** — a raw volume presented to a machine as if it were a local disk, reached over iSCSI. Think of a database that wants its own dedicated disk.
- **Object storage** — data addressed by key over an S3-compatible interface, for large numbers of unstructured items. Think of backups, media, or application data that does not fit a folder tree.

If a scenario says "shared folder", "dedicated volume", or "S3", that is the sorting key.

## Databases: the lifecycle, not the engine

A database product in this space is usually not a new database engine. It is software that handles the *lifecycle* around an existing engine: provisioning a new instance, cloning a copy for testing, taking backups, applying patches, and tearing instances down when they are finished. The problem it solves is the sheer number of manual, error-prone steps between "we need a test copy of production" and having one.

Listen for phrases about **provisioning, cloning, patching, or lifecycle** and you are in this family.

## Cloud management: cost, operations, and self-service

Once you have several clusters, someone has to answer questions that no single cluster can answer on its own: what is everything costing, is it configured consistently, who is allowed to request what. A cloud-management product is the layer that aggregates those concerns — reporting on cost and capacity, checking configuration against a policy, and giving teams a self-service catalogue so they can request an environment without a ticket.

Listen for **cost, compliance, policy, or self-service portal**.

## Containers: the orchestration platform

Containers package an application and its dependencies so it runs the same way everywhere. Running a few by hand is easy; running hundreds across many machines needs an orchestrator to schedule them, restart the ones that fail, and expose them to the network. A Kubernetes platform product provides that orchestrator, managed, on top of your infrastructure.

Listen for **container, Kubernetes, cluster orchestration, or microservices**.

## AI and end-user computing

Two more families round out the picture:

- **AI workloads** need accelerators, large datasets, and a way to serve a model to users. An AI product family provides the infrastructure and tooling for training and inference rather than making you assemble it from virtual machines.
- **End-user computing** delivers a desktop to a person rather than a server to an application — virtual desktops streamed to a thin client, a laptop, or a browser. The problem it solves is managing hundreds of desktops centrally instead of one machine at a time.

Listen for **model, GPU, inference** for the first, and **virtual desktop, VDI, or streamed desktop** for the second.

## Worked example: which family solves this?

Sort each statement to a family. Say the reasoning out loud; that is the exercise.

1. "Our testers need a fresh copy of the production database every Friday." — *Database lifecycle.* The chore is cloning, repeatedly, on a schedule.
2. "We have no idea what our three clusters cost us per department." — *Cloud management.* The chore is cost visibility across clusters.
3. "The development team wants to request a three-node environment themselves." — *Cloud management.* Self-service catalogue.
4. "An application needs a 2 TB volume it can format itself." — *Block storage.* A dedicated volume, presented as a disk.
5. "We need to archive five years of scanned documents cheaply." — *Object storage.* Many unstructured items, addressed by key.
6. "Two hundred contractors need a standard desktop on day one." — *End-user computing.* Desktops delivered to people, centrally managed.
7. "A data-science team wants to serve a model to an internal web page." — *AI.* Serving a model is inference.
8. "The new service ships as container images and must survive a node reboot." — *Containers.* Orchestration and rescheduling.
