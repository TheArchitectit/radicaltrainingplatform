# Alerts and Events (S4.2)

*Module S4.2 — Nutanix NCA 7.5 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

A health check is something you ask for. An alert is something the cluster tells you. This lesson covers the difference between an alert and an event, how severity works, what acknowledging an alert actually means, and — the skill this objective is really testing — where to look first when several things are shouting at once.

## Alert versus event

These two are used interchangeably in ordinary conversation and mean different things here.

An **event** is a record that something happened. The cluster keeps a log of them: a node came back, a task completed, a configuration changed. Events are a history. Most of them are entirely routine, and their value is in reconstructing what happened, especially after the fact.

An **alert** is a condition that has been raised because it needs attention. An alert has a severity, it persists until the condition clears or someone resolves it, and it represents the cluster's judgement that a human should probably look.

The relationship between them is the useful part: **alerts are generated from events**. A single event may be unremarkable; a pattern of events, or an event that crosses a threshold, becomes an alert. So when you are diagnosing, the alerts tell you *what* is wrong and the events tell you *what happened* — and the events usually contain the sequence you need.

## Severity

Alerts carry a severity, and the severity is a statement about urgency rather than about difficulty.

- **Critical** — something is broken or about to be. This is the level that wakes people up.
- **Warning** — something is approaching a limit, or a condition exists that will become critical if it continues.
- **Informational** — something happened that is worth recording. Informational alerts are the boundary between alert and event.

The trap is treating the list as a to-do queue to be worked top to bottom. A single critical alert usually matters more than a dozen warnings, and a warning that has been sitting unchanged for a month may matter more than a critical alert that resolved itself in thirty seconds.

## Acknowledging: what it does and does not do

**Acknowledging** an alert means "a human has seen this and is aware of it". It is a statement about *attention*, not about *state*.

That distinction is the whole point, and it is a favourite exam topic. Acknowledging an alert:

- **Does** stop it from being treated as unexamined, and records that someone has taken responsibility for it.
- **Does not** fix anything, and **does not** clear the underlying condition. If the condition is still true, the alert remains true; you have only recorded that you know.

The failure this prevents: acknowledging an alert to get it out of the way, then forgetting that the thing it described is still happening. Acknowledged-but-unresolved is a state worth being suspicious of when you review an environment.

## Where to look first

Given a cluster with several alerts, this is the order that works:

1. **Critical first — but read all of them before acting.** Critical alerts are where the damage is, but alerts are often *related*: one root cause produces a cluster of symptoms. Reading the whole set before acting is how you avoid fixing five symptoms of one problem.
2. **Then the oldest unresolved.** An alert that has been open for days is either being ignored or is genuinely stuck, and both are worth knowing about.
3. **Then warnings that are trending.** A capacity warning that is moving is a warning about next week, not today.
4. **Then the acknowledged ones.** Confirm the acknowledgement was deserved, because an acknowledged alert that nobody is actually working on is invisible by design.

The single most useful habit: **look for the common cause before the individual fixes**. A cluster reporting that several virtual machines are unreachable, plus a storage alert, plus a network warning, is usually one problem with three faces — and the alert that appeared *first* is often the one that explains the rest.

## Reading a single alert properly

For any one alert, four questions give you what you need:

- **What is the severity?** Urgency, not difficulty.
- **When did it start?** The start time is how you correlate it with events and with changes someone made.
- **Is it still active, or has it resolved?** A resolved alert is history; an active one is a current condition.
- **What else appeared at the same time?** Correlation is the fastest route to a cause.
