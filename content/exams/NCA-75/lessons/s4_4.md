# Workload Performance (S4.4)

*Module S4.4 — Nutanix NCA 7.5 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

"Slow" is the vaguest word in infrastructure support and the most common complaint. This lesson is about turning it into something you can act on: reading the performance charts, and distinguishing the two situations that produce the same complaint but need opposite responses — a machine that is being starved by a neighbour, and a cluster that is genuinely out of capacity.

## Reading a performance chart

A performance chart has three parts, and beginners tend to read only the middle one.

- **The level** — how much of something is being used. This is what people look at, and on its own it is the least informative part.
- **The trend** — whether the level is climbing, flat, or cycling. A level that is climbing is a warning about the future even when it is currently comfortable.
- **The shape** — the pattern within the window. A steady line and a line that spikes every ten minutes can have the same average and mean entirely different things.

So the first habit is to read the trend and the shape, not just the number. "Storage is 70% full" is not useful on its own; "storage is 70% full and climbing about 2% a week" is a plan.

## Two complaints, two causes

The same sentence — "the application is slow" — has two fundamentally different causes, and telling them apart is this objective's core skill.

**Cause one: a capacity ceiling.** The resource genuinely is exhausted. Storage is full, the processors are saturated, memory is over-committed. Everyone on that resource is affected, and the fix is to add capacity or reduce demand.

**Cause two: contention from a neighbour.** The resource has plenty of headroom *in total*, but one workload is consuming a disproportionate share, so others are starved. This is the **noisy neighbour** problem. The cluster looks healthy by the numbers, the affected user is suffering, and the fix is about the *offending workload*, not about capacity.

The distinguishing question is: **is the resource constrained in total, or is the constraint specific to the affected workload?** If the cluster has headroom while one machine is slow, you are looking at contention. If everything on the resource is slow, you are looking at a ceiling.

## Recognising a noisy neighbour

The symptoms of contention are specific enough to recognise:

- **One workload is slow while others are normal.** The clearest signal. If it were a ceiling, everything on that resource would feel it.
- **The slowness is periodic, not constant.** Contention follows the neighbour's activity, so it appears when the neighbour is busy and eases when it is not. A constant symptom suggests a ceiling; an intermittent one suggests contention.
- **The aggregate numbers look fine.** Total processor or storage use is unremarkable, because the resource is not full — it is just unevenly shared.
- **The timing correlates with something else.** A backup window, a report that runs hourly, a batch job that starts at midnight. When you can correlate the symptom with another workload's schedule, you have found the neighbour.

The trap is treating this as a capacity problem and adding hardware, which does not help: the neighbour will consume the new headroom too.

## Recognising a ceiling

The symptoms of a genuine ceiling are the mirror image:

- **Everything on the resource is affected**, not one workload.
- **The symptom is continuous** and tends to worsen over time as the level climbs.
- **The aggregate numbers are high**, consistently, not just at peaks.
- **The fix is capacity or demand**: add resource, or reduce what is asking for it.

## From symptom to evidence

The workflow that turns a complaint into an action:

1. **Establish what "slow" means.** Which application, which operation, since when, for whom.
2. **Isolate whether it is one workload or all of them** on the shared resource. This single question splits the two causes.
3. **Read the trend, not just the level.** Is this a new condition or a long-standing one that has crossed a line?
4. **Correlate with other activity.** Another workload's schedule, a recent change, a maintenance window.
5. **Only then decide** whether the answer is capacity, a change to the offending workload, or something else entirely.

Step 2 is where most people go wrong, because they skip straight from the complaint to a solution.
