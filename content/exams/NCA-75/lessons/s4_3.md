# Support Functions (S4.3)

*Module S4.3 — Nutanix NCA 7.5 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Some problems you fix yourself. Some need the vendor's help, and the difference between a support case that is resolved quickly and one that drags is usually how well it was prepared. This lesson covers the three parts of that: the telemetry that runs continuously, the collection of logs you gather on demand, and the discipline of opening a case with the right material attached.

## Pulse: the continuous channel

**Pulse** is the telemetry service. It periodically sends diagnostic and health information from the cluster back to the vendor, so that the vendor has visibility into environments without anyone having to ask for it.

Three things follow from that, and they are the recognition points:

- Pulse is **automatic and continuous**, not something you run. If a question says "we need to send information to the vendor now", Pulse is not the answer — collection is.
- Pulse is **outbound telemetry**, so in a dark site (section 3.3) it may be disabled or proxied. An environment that has never sent telemetry is a normal configuration, not a fault.
- Pulse supports **proactive** support: the vendor can sometimes see a developing problem before the customer reports it. That is its purpose, and it is why disabling it changes the support relationship rather than just saving bandwidth.

## Collecting logs on demand

When a specific problem needs investigation, you gather the relevant logs from the cluster and attach them to the case. The tool for this is **Collector** (section 1.3) — it produces a packaged report of the environment's configuration and state.

The skill here is scoping the collection. A collection can be narrow — the logs for one component, over a specific window — or broad. Two rules:

- **Match the window to the problem.** If the problem started at 14:00 yesterday, a collection covering the last hour is useless. The start time of the alert (section 4.2) tells you what window to ask for.
- **Narrow first.** A broad collection is slow to produce and slow for the vendor to work through. Start with the component that the evidence points at, and widen only if it is not enough.

This is where the earlier lessons pay off: to scope a collection you need to know which component is implicated, and that comes from reading the alerts and events properly rather than guessing.

## Opening a support case

A case is a conversation, and the quality of the first message sets its trajectory. The material that makes a case good:

- **What is the symptom, in observable terms.** "The cluster is slow" is not a symptom. "Virtual machines on node three respond in seconds rather than milliseconds, since 14:00 yesterday" is.
- **When it started, and what changed around then.** The single highest-value piece of information. Most problems have a change near their start — a maintenance window, a configuration edit, a new workload.
- **What you have already checked.** Saying what you ruled out stops the vendor from asking you to repeat it, and it demonstrates you have done the diagnostic work.
- **The evidence attached.** The collection, scoped to the window.
- **The impact, stated plainly.** What is not working, and for whom. This is what determines how the case is prioritised.

The failure this prevents: a case that opens with "it is broken", gets a request for information, and spends its first day in a round trip that could have been avoided.

## Working the case

Two habits worth building:

- **Keep the case updated with what you observe**, not only with what you want. The sequence of what changed and when is often what leads to the answer, and it is much harder to reconstruct later.
- **Apply and verify the resolution.** A case closed because a fix was suggested is not a case closed because the problem is gone. Verify, then close.

## An honest limit

The lab simulator for this track does not simulate the vendor's support systems. You cannot open a real case in it. Where a practice item or scenario touches the support workflow, it is a **labelled worked example** — you reason through how a case should be prepared and see the reasoning explained. Nothing here claims to have let you perform a support action you did not perform.
