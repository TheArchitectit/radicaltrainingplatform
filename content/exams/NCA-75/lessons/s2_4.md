# Licensing (S2.4)

*Module S2.4 — Nutanix NCA 7.5 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Licensing is the least glamorous part of the platform and the part most likely to surprise you in production, because a licensing problem rarely looks like a licensing problem. This lesson stays at recognition level: what a licence is attached to, where you see it, and what happens when the environment outgrows what was bought.

## What a licence is attached to

The important idea is that a licence is tied to the **capacity of the hardware**, not to the number of virtual machines you run on it. You do not pay per virtual machine; you licence the compute the cluster is made of.

Two consequences follow, and both are exam-relevant:

- Adding a node to a cluster is not free from a licensing point of view. New capacity may need to be covered.
- Running more virtual machines on hardware you already own does not, by itself, change the licensing position.

So when a question describes a cluster growing, the licensing question is about the *hardware that was added*, not about the workloads that were deployed.

## Tiers, at recognition level

Licences come in tiers, and the tiers differ by which capabilities they unlock rather than by how much hardware they cover. A lower tier gives you the platform's core functions; higher tiers add the management, automation, and advanced data-services features.

You are not expected to memorise a price list or a feature matrix. The recognition skill is:

- If a scenario needs an advanced capability and the licence does not include it, the answer is about the *tier*, not about a broken configuration.
- If a scenario is about capacity, the answer is about the *amount of hardware covered*, not about the tier.

That split — capability versus capacity — resolves most licensing questions at this level.

## Where licensing lives

Licensing is visible and managed from **Prism**, the same interface you use for everything else. From there you can see what is licensed, what the environment is using, and whether anything is out of step.

The reason this matters is diagnostic. Because licensing information lives in the management plane, an alert about licensing appears there rather than in the guest operating system. A user reporting that "something is wrong with the cluster" will not see a licensing message on their own machine; the administrator sees it in Prism.

## When capacity is exceeded

Environments drift. A node is added and nobody updates the records; a cluster is rebuilt and the licence is not reapplied; a test environment is promoted to production. The result is an environment whose licensed capacity and actual capacity no longer agree.

What happens next depends on the direction and the size of the gap, but the shape is consistent:

- The management plane **reports** the discrepancy. This is the normal first symptom — a compliance or capacity notice in Prism rather than an outage.
- If the gap persists, **functionality can become restricted**. The platform protects itself in a defined way rather than simply failing without explanation.
- The fix is to **bring the licence into line** with what is actually deployed, not to remove hardware to match the paperwork.

The habit worth building: when an environment behaves in a way that no configuration change explains, and a notice is sitting in Prism that nobody read, read the notice.

## A worked example, and an honest limit

Consider this statement: *"A four-node cluster was expanded to six nodes last quarter. The administrator reports that several management features stopped working this week."*

Work through it: the change was to **capacity** (two nodes added). The symptom is about **capability** (features stopped working). Those are different questions, and the sequence to check is: is the licence covering the two new nodes, and does the current tier include the features that stopped working? Both are answered in Prism, not on the nodes.

This is a reasoning example, and it is deliberately only that. The lab simulator for this track does not simulate licence mutations — you cannot buy a tier or exceed a capacity limit in it. Where a scenario or practice item touches licensing, it is presented as a **labelled worked example**: you reason through the situation and see the answer explained. Nothing in this track pretends to have let you perform a licensing change you did not perform.
