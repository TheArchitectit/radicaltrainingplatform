# Health Checks (S4.1)

*Module S4.1 — Nutanix NCA 7.5 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

A health check is how you find out whether a cluster is well before a user tells you it is not. This lesson covers what the check examines, how to run it, and — the part beginners get wrong — how to read the results without treating every line as an emergency.

## The tool: Nutanix Cluster Check

**Nutanix Cluster Check (NCC)** is the health-check tool. It runs a collection of individual checks against the cluster, each examining one aspect of its state, and produces a report of what it found.

The mental model that matters: NCC is not one test. It is many small tests, each answering a specific question — is this component healthy, is that configuration what it should be, is anything approaching a limit. Understanding that is what makes the output readable, because you get a list of findings rather than a single verdict.

## What it checks

The checks cluster into a few groups, and knowing the groups tells you what a finding is likely to mean:

- **Hardware and component state** — disks, nodes, power supplies, and similar physical elements. A finding here is often something the cluster already knew about and has already compensated for.
- **Software and configuration** — whether versions and settings match what is expected for this environment.
- **Capacity and utilisation** — how full the storage is, how busy the cluster is, and how close either is to a threshold.
- **Health of the cluster's own services** — whether the internal components that make up the distributed system are all doing their jobs.

Most findings are informational. The ones that matter are the ones that indicate something **degraded** — a component that has failed and been compensated for, or a resource approaching a limit.

## How to run it

A health check is run on demand, and it is also run automatically as part of routine operation. The practical difference is why you are running it:

- **Routine** — a periodic check to confirm nothing has drifted. Read the report for anything degraded or approaching a threshold.
- **Before a change** — the pre-check discipline from sections 3.2 and 3.3. You are confirming the cluster is fit to have maintenance performed on it.
- **After a change** — confirming that whatever you did left the cluster in a healthy state.
- **When investigating a problem** — a health check is often the first thing to run, because it can surface a degraded component that nobody had noticed and that explains the symptom.

That last use is the one worth internalising: a health check is a diagnostic tool, not only a periodic ritual.

## Reading the results without panic

This is the skill the objective is really about. A health check on a working cluster will often produce findings, and the wrong response is to treat each one as a failure.

Read in this order:

1. **Is anything degraded?** A degraded component means something has failed and the cluster is compensating. This is the highest-value finding, even if the cluster is currently performing normally — it means you are running without margin.
2. **Is anything approaching a limit?** Storage or compute nearing a threshold is a warning about the future rather than a fault now. It is the finding most often ignored and most often the cause of a later incident.
3. **Is anything inconsistent?** A configuration that does not match what is expected, or a version that is out of step, usually points at something that was changed and not finished.
4. **Everything else.** Informational lines are informational. Read them, do not act on them reflexively.

The discipline is to separate "the cluster is broken" from "the cluster is fine and one of its components is being carried". Those are very different states, and conflating them leads to unnecessary emergency work.

## The habit worth building

Run the check before you need it. The value of a health check is highest when you have a baseline to compare against — you know what normal looks like for this environment, so you can tell which findings are new. A health check run for the first time during an incident tells you the cluster is unhealthy; it cannot tell you what changed.
