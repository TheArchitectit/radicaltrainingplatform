# Software Maintenance (S3.3)

*Module S3.3 — Nutanix NCA 7.5 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Planned software maintenance means upgrading the layers the cluster runs on: the hypervisor, the cluster software, and the firmware underneath. This lesson covers the workflow at concept level. The recurring theme is that the tool does most of the work, and your job is the judgement around it — deciding what to update, in what order, and whether now is the right time.

## The tool: Life Cycle Manager

**Life Cycle Manager (LCM)** is the tool that discovers, stages, and applies updates across the cluster. It is the answer to "how do I keep this environment current" in a Nutanix context, and its value is that it treats the cluster as one system rather than as a pile of separate components to be updated by hand.

What LCM does, in order of the workflow:

- **Inventory** — find out what versions are installed and what updates are available.
- **Compatibility check** — determine whether a given update is compatible with everything else in this specific environment.
- **Pre-checks** — validate that the cluster is in a fit state to be updated right now.
- **Apply** — perform the update, one component at a time, in an order it determines.

The one-click upgrade people talk about is the last step. The value is in the first three.

## Inventory and compatibility

The **inventory** step answers "what do I have, and what could I have". LCM collects the versions of the software and firmware in the environment and compares them against available updates.

The **compatibility** step is the one that saves you. The layers of a cluster are interdependent: a hypervisor version, a cluster software version, and a firmware version form a combination, and not every combination is supported. Updating one component without regard for the others can produce a configuration that runs but is unsupported — which means that when something goes wrong later, you are on your own.

So the correct sequence is never "update the thing that is out of date". It is "let the tool tell me which updates are compatible with *this* environment, then choose among those".

## Pre-checks: the same discipline as hardware maintenance

**Pre-checks** are the software equivalent of confirming cluster health before evacuating a node. Before applying anything, LCM validates that the environment is in a state where the update can succeed: the cluster is healthy, no other operation is in flight, there is enough free capacity to absorb the rolling process, and the components are where they need to be.

A failed pre-check is not an obstacle to be overridden. It is the tool telling you that something else needs attention first. The discipline from section 3.2 applies unchanged: fix the underlying condition, then come back.

## What "rolling" means

Updates are applied one node at a time rather than all at once, because applying them everywhere simultaneously would take the cluster down. The pattern is: take one node out of service, update it, bring it back, confirm it is healthy, move to the next. During this, the workloads on each node move to the others — the same evacuation idea from section 3.2, performed by the tool.

Two consequences follow. First, an upgrade **takes time** proportional to the number of nodes, and the cluster runs on reduced capacity throughout. Second, an upgrade **needs headroom**: if the cluster is already tight on resources, there may not be room to run everything on the remaining nodes while one is being updated. That is why "how much free capacity do we have" is part of the pre-check.

## Dark-site awareness

Not every environment can reach the outside world. A **dark site** has no internet access — a common situation in secure or remote facilities. This matters for maintenance because update tooling often expects to download what it needs.

The practical point at this level is recognition: in a dark site, updates must be brought in through whatever controlled channel exists rather than fetched directly, and that has to be arranged *before* the maintenance window, not during it. If a question describes an isolated environment and an upgrade, the answer involves planning the transfer of the update media, not troubleshooting a download.

## Judgement around the tool

LCM handles the mechanics. The judgement is yours:

- **What should be updated now, and what can wait?** Not every available update is urgent.
- **Is this a good time?** A rolling upgrade runs on reduced capacity for a while, so it should not coincide with a period of peak demand or with other maintenance.
- **Is the environment in a fit state?** That is what pre-checks answer, and the answer is not negotiable.

The exam usually tests the sequencing and the discipline rather than the interface: check compatibility, check the cluster, then apply.
