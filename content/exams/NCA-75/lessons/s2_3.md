# Storage Operations (S2.3)

*Module S2.3 — Nutanix NCA 7.5 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Every virtual machine you create has a disk, and that disk lives somewhere in the cluster's storage. This lesson explains where, what protects it, and the four techniques the cluster uses to make the space go further. It stays at concept level throughout: you are learning what the knobs do and when you would reach for them, not how to turn them.

## Containers: where disks live

A **storage container** is a logical group of storage on the cluster, with its own set of policies. When you create a virtual machine, its disk is placed in a container. The container is where you set the rules that apply to everything inside it — how many copies are kept, whether compression or deduplication is on, and similar.

The useful mental model is a folder with a policy attached. You do not usually create many containers. You create a small number that reflect genuinely different needs — perhaps one for ordinary virtual machines and one for something that needs different protection — and then place disks into the right one.

The consequence to remember: changing a container's policy applies to the disks inside it. That is why a container is a considered object rather than something you create freely.

## Capacity: what you bought and what you can use

A cluster's storage is not simply the sum of the disks you installed. Several things come out of the headline number before a virtual machine can use it:

- **The hypervisor and cluster software occupy space** on each node.
- **Protection copies** consume space, as the next section explains.
- **Reserved headroom** is kept so the cluster can absorb a failure and still function.

So the number that matters is **usable capacity**, not raw disk capacity, and the two can be surprisingly far apart. A cluster that appears half empty by disk count may be much closer to full in terms of what it will actually accept.

## Replication factor: how many copies

**Replication factor (RF)** is how many copies of your data the cluster keeps. The two you will meet are:

- **RF2** — two copies of every piece of data, on two different nodes. This survives the loss of one node. It uses roughly twice the space of the data itself.
- **RF3** — three copies, on three different nodes. This survives the loss of two nodes at once, and costs roughly three times the space.

Because the copies are placed on *different* nodes, the protection is real: losing a node does not lose the data, because a complete copy exists elsewhere. And because the copies occupy space, RF3 halves the usable capacity of a given set of disks compared with RF2.

The decision is a trade between how much failure you must survive and how much space you are willing to spend on it. RF3 is chosen where losing two nodes at once must be survivable; RF2 is the usual choice where one is enough. Section 3.1 returns to this as a cluster-design question.

## Data efficiency: four techniques

These four reduce how much space your data actually occupies. Each has a "when would you" answer.

**Compression.** Compression finds repeated patterns within data and stores them more compactly. It works best on data that is compressible — text, logs, many application files — and does little for data that is already compressed, such as images or archives. *When would you reach for it:* broadly, because it is cheap; but do not expect a dramatic saving on already-compressed content.

**Deduplication.** Deduplication finds identical blocks and stores one copy, with the others pointing at it. It shines where the same data exists many times — cloned virtual machines, repeated operating system files, backup sets. *When would you reach for it:* in environments with many similar machines, which is most virtualised environments.

**Erasure coding.** Erasure coding stores data plus calculated redundancy spread across nodes, so that a missing piece can be reconstructed. It provides protection similar to replication while consuming noticeably less space, at the cost of more computation and a different performance profile. *When would you reach for it:* for cold or archival data, where space matters more than the speed of an access.

**Thin provisioning.** Space is allocated to a disk when the disk is *written*, not when it is created. A 500 GB disk that holds 50 GB consumes 50 GB. *When would you reach for it:* always, by default — but remember that it makes "the disk is only using 10%" a statement about allocation, not about what is genuinely free, because a hundred such disks can all grow at once.

## Reading a capacity question

When a question gives you numbers, it is usually testing one of two things: whether you accounted for **protection copies** (RF2 means roughly double), or whether you confused **allocated** space with **used** space. Before doing arithmetic, ask which of the two the question is really about.
