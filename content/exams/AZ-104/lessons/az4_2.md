# Configure secure access to virtual networks (S4.2)

*Module S4.2 — Azure Administrator AZ-104 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Routing gets a packet to the right place; security decides whether it is allowed to arrive. This lesson builds one picture: a **network security group** is an ordered allow/deny list attached to a boundary, and everything else here — application security groups, Bastion, service and private endpoints — is a refinement of that idea or a way to avoid exposing a boundary at all.

## Network security groups and their rules

An **NSG** contains rules evaluated in **priority order**, lowest number first. The first rule that matches the traffic decides the outcome and evaluation stops. Each rule has:

- a **priority** (100–4096, lower wins)
- a **source** and **destination** (an IP range, a service tag such as `Internet` or `VirtualNetwork`, or an application security group)
- a **port** (or range, or `*`)
- a **protocol** (TCP, UDP, ICMP, or Any)
- an **action** — Allow or Deny

Every NSG ships with **default rules** you cannot delete, numbered 65000, 65001 and 65500. They allow traffic within the virtual network and from Azure Load Balancer, allow outbound internet traffic, and finish with a **deny-all inbound** rule. That final rule is why a new NSG with no custom rules blocks inbound traffic from the internet.

Two properties cause most confusion. NSGs are **stateful** — if you allow inbound TCP 443, the reply traffic is permitted automatically without a matching outbound rule. And an NSG can be attached at the **subnet** or at the **NIC**; if both apply, traffic must be allowed by both. For inbound traffic the subnet NSG is evaluated first, then the NIC NSG.

**Application security groups (ASGs)** let you group NICs by role — `web-servers`, `db-servers` — and then write a rule whose source is an ASG rather than an IP range. When you add a VM to the group, it inherits the rule; you never edit the rule again. **Effective security rules** is the view that shows, for one NIC, the combined result of every subnet and NIC NSG after defaults are merged — the right place to look when a rule you wrote does not seem to apply.

## Bastion and private access to platform services

**Azure Bastion** gives you browser-based RDP and SSH to a VM through the Azure portal. Its point is that the VM needs **no public IP address** at all; Bastion is deployed into a `AzureBastionSubnet` in your VNet and brokers the session. This removes the exposed management port that would otherwise be the most attacked surface on the VM.

For platform services such as storage accounts, SQL databases and key vaults, there are two ways to reach them privately, and the distinction is a favourite question.

- A **service endpoint** extends your VNet identity to the service. Your subnet keeps using the service's public address, but Azure adds a route so the traffic stays on the Microsoft backbone, and the service can be locked to that subnet. The service still has a public IP.
- A **private endpoint** places a **private IP address from your own VNet** in front of the service. The resource is then reachable at that private address, and you can disable public access entirely. Private endpoints also work across peered networks and from on-premises over VPN or ExpressRoute.

Rule of thumb: service endpoint when you want a cheaper, simpler lockdown and the service can keep a public address; private endpoint when the requirement is that no public path exists.

## Worked example: three access problems

1. **A VM in a peered VNet is denied access to a storage account that allows only a service endpoint from the original subnet.** Service endpoints are scoped to the subnet, not the VNet as a whole, and peering does not extend them. Either add the peered subnet's endpoint and to the storage firewall, or move to a private endpoint, which does work across peering.
2. **You add an inbound NSG rule allowing TCP 3389 from your office, but RDP still fails.** Check whether a second NSG is attached at the other boundary. Both the subnet NSG and the NIC NSG must allow the traffic; a deny in either one wins. The effective security rules view shows exactly which rule denied it.
3. **An administrator needs RDP to twenty VMs and the security team will not allow public IPs on them.** Deploy Azure Bastion into an `AzureBastionSubnet` and connect through the portal. The VMs keep only private addresses, and no inbound management port is exposed.
