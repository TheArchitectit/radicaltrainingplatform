# Manage access to Azure resources (S1.2)

*Module S1.2 — Azure Administrator AZ-104 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Identity answers "who are you". This lesson answers "what may you do" — and the picture to hold is a sentence with three parts: **a principal**, **a role**, and **a scope**. Every access question in Azure resolves to that sentence, and every access problem is one of those three parts being wrong.

## The three parts of a role assignment

A **role assignment** binds a **security principal** (a user, a group, a service principal, or a managed identity), a **role definition** (the list of permitted actions), and a **scope** (where the assignment applies).

A role definition is itself just a list. It names `Actions` that are permitted and `NotActions` that are carved out, and for storage-style services it also names `DataActions`, which govern the data inside a resource rather than the resource itself. Because a role is data, Azure can ship hundreds of built-in roles and you can write your own.

Four built-in roles cover most beginner scenarios:

- **Owner** — full management of everything at the scope, *including* granting access to others.
- **Contributor** — full management of resources at the scope, but **cannot grant access** to anyone.
- **Reader** — view only.
- **User Access Administrator** — manage *who has access*, without managing the resources themselves.

The Owner/Contributor gap is the most useful distinction to internalise: Contributor is deliberately unable to escalate, because it cannot hand its power to someone else.

## Scope and inheritance

Azure organises resources into a hierarchy: **management group → subscription → resource group → resource**. An assignment made higher up is **inherited** by everything beneath it, including resources created tomorrow.

**Effective access** is the union of everything inherited plus everything assigned directly. Access is additive: if a user is Reader at the subscription and Contributor at one resource group, then inside that resource group they are a Contributor. Being a Reader somewhere never subtracts from a Contributor grant elsewhere.

The one thing that does subtract is a **deny assignment**, which takes precedence over any allow no matter how senior the role. You do not create them by hand in the portal — Azure creates them on your behalf when a managed application or similar construct needs to protect resources it owns. So when a user with Owner still cannot touch a resource, "look for a deny assignment" is the right instinct.

## Azure RBAC roles versus Entra directory roles

These are two different systems that share a portal, and mixing them up produces confident wrong answers.

- **Azure RBAC roles** govern *Azure resources*: virtual machines, storage accounts, resource groups, subscriptions.
- **Microsoft Entra directory roles** govern *directory objects*: creating users, resetting passwords, managing groups and app registrations. Global Administrator and User Administrator live here.

A Global Administrator is not automatically an Owner of your subscriptions — the role can elevate itself to gain that access, but it is not the same grant. And being Owner of a subscription grants nothing over the directory.

## Worked example: three small decisions

1. **A developer who is Contributor on a resource group cannot give a colleague access.** That is by design. Either grant the developer **User Access Administrator** at that resource group, or have an Owner make the assignment. Widening Contributor changes nothing.
2. **An auditor is Reader on the subscription but needs to manage resources in one resource group.** Assign **Contributor** at that resource group. The inherited Reader is not a barrier; the two grants combine, and the wider one wins inside the scope.
3. **A group needs to read storage blobs, and only blobs.** A resource-level role such as Reader will not do it — reading blob *data* requires a role whose definition includes the relevant data actions, assigned at the storage account or below.
