# Operational Procedures (B4)

*Module B4 — CompTIA A+ Core 2 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Support work is not only about fixing machines. It is about leaving a trail someone else can follow, changing production systems on purpose instead of by accident, and keeping copies of data that matter. This lesson covers the four habits that make that possible: writing a useful ticket, backing up on purpose, changing things on purpose, and talking to a frustrated user without making the situation worse.

## What a ticket is for

A ticket is a **communication record** and a **history** at the same time. It is how the next technician — who may be you in six months — learns what happened without re-diagnosing from zero.

A useful ticket answers four questions:

1. **What did the user report?** Their words first, then your restatement of the symptom.
2. **What did you already try?** Every test, including the ones that ruled things out. "Nothing works" is not a finding; "ping to the gateway succeeds, nslookup fails" is.
3. **What was actually wrong?** The cause, not just the action. "Fixed the network" is empty; "workstation DNS pointed at a decommissioned server" is a cause.
4. **What did you change, and how did you verify it?** The change, and the proof the user's own workflow works again.

`Fixed it.` is not a resolution note. A resolution note is what you would want to read if you were picking this ticket up cold.

## Backup basics (3-2-1)

Backups only matter when they are the copy that survives the failure. The **3-2-1** idea is a concept-level rule of thumb:

- **3** copies of the data (the live copy plus at least two backups)
- **2** different media or storage types (so one class of failure does not take every copy)
- **1** copy offsite or otherwise separated from the live environment (so theft, fire, or ransomware on the desk does not take every copy)

An external drive that stays plugged into the same desk is a second copy, not a backup plan. If the machine is encrypted by ransomware, or the desk is stolen, that drive goes with it. A backup you have never restored from is a guess, not a backup.

## Change control: write it down first

Change control sounds formal. At beginner scale it is one habit: **write down what you will do before you do it.**

Before changing something that other people depend on — a DHCP scope, a switch port, a DNS forwarder — capture:

- what you are changing and why
- what you expect to happen
- how you will tell whether it worked
- how you will put it back if it does not

That short note is what turns a risky tweak into a planned change. If something breaks at 9 a.m., the note is the difference between "we changed the DNS server at 8:55 and here is the rollback" and "something is wrong and nobody knows what moved."

Approval is the organizational half of the same idea: people who own the system know the change is coming.

## Safety and privacy habits

Two habits cover a lot of ground:

- **Do not collect or carry data you do not need.** Screenshots of a user's desktop, exported mailboxes, and "temporary" copies of files are how private data leaks. If you must capture evidence, capture the smallest thing that proves the point.
- **Dispose of storage on purpose.** Deleting a file, or even formatting a drive, does not reliably erase it. Disposal that actually protects data means wiping or destroying the media according to the organization's procedure.

## Talking to a frustrated user

The user is not the ticket. They are a person whose day is blocked.

- **Listen first, diagnose second.** Let them finish the symptom description before you start testing.
- **Restate what you heard** in plain language. It confirms you understand and it slows the conversation down to a workable pace.
- **Say what you will do next, and when you will update them.** Uncertainty is often more stressful than the outage.
- **Never guess out loud.** "It might be the server, or maybe the switch" creates fear and is not a theory. Form a theory, test it, then speak.

You can be warm and still be precise. Precision is a form of respect: it means you took their problem seriously enough to describe it correctly.
