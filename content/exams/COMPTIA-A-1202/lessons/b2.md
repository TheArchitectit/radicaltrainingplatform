# Security and Ticketing Basics (B2)

*Module B2 — CompTIA A+ Core 2 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

B1 configured the machine. This module makes it safe to own. Security at the A+ level is
mostly about defaults, hygiene and people: accounts with too much power, passwords shared
in a chat, wireless with no encryption, a user who clicks the link. The exam asks you to
name the control, choose it for a scenario, and — in the ticketing half — say what you
record when a security incident lands on you.

## Security measures: physical, logical and human

Every control falls into one of three groups:

- **Physical.** Locked rooms and cabinets, cable locks, badge access, cameras, locked
  racks. A stolen laptop with an unencrypted disk is a data breach with a handle.
- **Logical.** Authentication, permissions, encryption, firewalls, endpoint protection,
  patching. The controls configured inside the operating system.
- **Administrative.** Policy, training, least privilege, separation of duties, an asset
  inventory, and an incident process that says who to call.

Three principles carry the reasoning. **Least privilege**: accounts get the access their
job needs and nothing more, which is why a technician does not work all day in an
administrator account. **Defense in depth**: several layers, so one failure does not end
the story. **Implicit deny**: when nothing grants access, access is refused — the safe
default.

## Windows security settings

The settings the exam names, and what each does:

- **Users and groups.** A **standard user** can do normal work; an **administrator**
  account can change the system. **UAC** is the prompt that puts a standard user's
  elevation in front of the person at the keyboard, and the reason "run as administrator"
  is a deliberate act instead of a habit.
- **NTFS permissions.** Full control, modify, read and execute, and read — assigned to
  users and groups, inherited by subfolders, and cumulative (the most permissive wins
  across groups). **Share permissions** apply at the network layer; the effective result
  is the more restrictive of the two.
- **BitLocker.** Full-volume encryption, with a recovery key that must be stored somewhere
  a locked-out user can reach. **BitLocker To Go** does the same for removable drives.
- **Windows Defender and the firewall.** Endpoint protection for malware and the host
  firewall for network traffic, both configurable by profile (domain, private, public).
- **Password policy and account lockout.** Length and complexity requirements, an expiry
  if the organization uses one, and a lockout threshold after repeated failures — the
  specific control against brute force and password spraying.
- **Security logs and auditing.** What Event Viewer's security log records, and the
  reminder that the log is evidence only if the clock is right and the log is retained.

## Wireless security and authentication

The evolution is the exam's favorite line:

- **WEP** — broken long ago. Never correct, always a wrong answer.
- **WPA** — a stopgap; TKIP encryption, superseded.
- **WPA2** — AES encryption (CCMP), and still the common baseline.
- **WPA3** — stronger key exchange and protection against offline guessing, with
  **WPA3-Enterprise** for authenticated users.
- **802.1X** — port-based authentication, where a **RADIUS** server checks the device or
  user before the network grants access. This is the enterprise answer; a shared
  pre-shared key is the small-office answer, and the trade is that everyone with the key
  is everyone.

Two practical notes: a **guest network with a separate password** is what keeps visitors
off the internal one, and **WPS** (the push-button or PIN pairing) should be disabled —
it is the easiest way into an otherwise well-configured wireless network.

## Malware: types and removal

Know the types by their behavior:

- **Virus** — attaches to a file and spreads when the file runs.
- **Worm** — spreads on its own across networks, no user action needed.
- **Trojan** — looks like something wanted, does something else.
- **Ransomware** — encrypts data and demands payment; offline backups are the answer that
  works.
- **Rootkit** — hides inside the operating system and conceals itself.
- **Spyware, adware, keylogger** — collects information, displays advertising, records
  keystrokes.
- **Botnet client** — turns the machine into a soldier in someone else's army.

The removal procedure has an order, and the order is the answer: **identify** the
infection; **quarantine** the infected system from the network; **disable System Restore**
so the infection cannot be restored from a save point; **remediate** by scanning, removing,
and repairing; **re-enable** System Restore and create a clean restore point; **update**
the OS and the security software; **scan again** to confirm; and **educate** the user about
how it arrived. When the infection is a rootkit or the system is no longer trustworthy,
**reimage** — it is faster and more certain than cleaning.

## Social engineering

The attacks target helpfulness, not software:

- **Phishing** — fraudulent e-mail; **spear phishing** — crafted for one target;
  **whaling** — aimed at executives.
- **Vishing** — a phone call; **smishing** — a text message.
- **Pretexting** — an invented story that justifies the request.
- **Baiting** — a tempting object, physical or digital; **tailgating** — following someone
  through a door.
- **Shoulder surfing**, **dumpster diving**, **impersonation**, **evil twin** wireless,
  **watering hole** sites, and **typosquatting** domains.

The manipulation levers to recognize: authority ("this is IT"), urgency ("your account
closes in ten minutes"), social proof ("everyone else has already confirmed"), and
familiarity (a message that knows your manager's name). The defenses are procedural, not
technical: verify through a second channel, never read a credential over the phone, and
make reporting a mistake safe — a user who confesses in ten minutes costs far less than
one who hides it for a week.

## Hardening: workstation, mobile and SOHO

**Workstations:** strong unique passwords, screen lock on idle, endpoint protection on,
automatic updates, host firewall on, autorun disabled, unused services and accounts
removed, and full-disk encryption on laptops.

**Mobile devices:** a passcode or biometric lock, device encryption (on by default),
**MDM** enrollment for company data, remote wipe configured and tested, updates applied,
applications from the platform's own store only, and rooting or jailbreaking treated as a
compliance failure because it removes the platform's own protections.

**SOHO wireless and routers:** change the default administrator password, use WPA2 or
WPA3, disable WPS and remote administration, name the network without identifying the
hardware or the resident, keep firmware updated, and separate guest traffic. **Content
filtering** and a **VPN** for remote workers are the two additions a small office usually
needs.

**Browser and data:** clear cache and cookies on shared machines, understand what private
browsing does and does not hide, keep extensions minimal, and heed certificate warnings —
a warning that appears only sometimes is often the more interesting event.

**Disposal.** Deleting a file and formatting a drive are not destruction. **Logical**
destruction overwrites the media or uses the drive's secure-erase command; **physical**
destruction shreds, drills, or degausses, and the choice depends on the media (degaussing
does nothing to flash). A **certificate of destruction** is what turns "we threw it out" into
a documented act.

## Tickets and security incidents

Security work is ticket work. A security incident ticket carries the same fields as any
other — symptom, what was tried, cause, action, verification — plus what was exposed, who
was notified, and what evidence was preserved. The B4 module covers the ticket discipline
in full; what belongs here is the rule that a suspected compromise is documented as it
happens, because the timeline is the part you cannot reconstruct later.
