# Hardware and Peripherals (A3)

*Module A3 — CompTIA A+ Core 1 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

This is the parts list. Every question in this module resolves to the same skill: knowing
what a component does, what it plugs into, what it costs in power or money, and which
number on the label actually matters. Learn the components by what they are for, and the
specifications stop being trivia.

## Displays: panels and the numbers that describe them

A display is a panel plus a signal path, and the exam asks about both.

- **Panel types.** **LCD** panels light the whole screen from behind and block light with
  liquid crystals; **IPS** is an LCD variant with wide viewing angles and better color,
  which is why it is the default for work that involves looking at the screen from an
  angle. **OLED** lights each pixel individually — true blacks, high contrast, thinner, and
  susceptible to burn-in when the same static image sits on screen for years. **Plasma** is
  legacy and effectively gone from the field.
- **Resolution and aspect ratio.** Resolution is the pixel grid (1920x1080, 2560x1440,
  3840x2160). Aspect ratio is its shape (16:9 widescreen, 16:10, 21:9 ultrawide, 4:3 on
  older projectors). Higher resolution on the same physical size means more workspace and
  smaller text; the operating system's scaling setting is what reconciles the two.
- **Refresh rate and response time.** Refresh rate (60 Hz, 120 Hz, 144 Hz) is how many
  times per second the panel redraws; response time is how fast a pixel changes. A monitor
  that advertises 144 Hz but is fed 60 Hz by a misconfigured cable is a classic ticket.
- **The attributes that decide a purchase:** contrast ratio, brightness in **nits**,
  color gamut, and whether the panel is matte or glossy.

**Connectors and cables** matter as much as the panel: **HDMI** carries video and audio
and is the common consumer connection; **DisplayPort** carries video and audio and, unlike
HDMI, supports daisy-chaining monitors; **DVI** is digital video only and legacy;
**VGA** is analog and belongs on the shelf marked "only if nothing else exists"; **USB-C**
can carry DisplayPort video if the port supports it — check for the DisplayPort or
Thunderbolt icon. Cable length and quality matter at higher resolutions: a marginal cable
that worked at 1080p produces dropouts at 4K.

## Cables, connectors and the physical layer

The skill here is matching a connector to its job:

- **Power connectors.** **SATA** power for drives; **Molex** for legacy accessories;
  **PCIe 8-pin and 6-pin** for graphics cards (a card that needs two 8-pin connectors will
  not run on one); **24-pin ATX** for the motherboard. Adapters exist and are how fires
  start.
- **Data connectors.** **SATA** data over a thin seven-pin cable for drives; **M.2** for
  NVMe and SATA solid-state drives mounted directly on the board; **eSATA** legacy
  external. **USB** in its versions (USB 2.0, 3.x, 4) and connector shapes (Type-A,
  Type-B, Type-C, Micro, Mini) — the version decides the speed, the shape decides whether
  it fits.
- **Network.** **RJ45** for Ethernet; **RJ11** for telephone; **fiber** connectors (LC,
  SC, ST) where the run is too long or too noisy for copper.
- **Video and audio.** HDMI, DisplayPort, DVI, VGA as above; **3.5 mm** analog audio
  jacks (line out, microphone) and **S/PDIF** for digital audio over optical or coaxial.

Two rules worth memorizing as rules: the connector shape does not guarantee the protocol
(a USB-C port can be USB 2.0), and the cable matters — a charge-only cable will not carry
data no matter what the ports support.

## Memory: the numbers that matter

RAM questions come down to four attributes:

- **Capacity** per module and per channel. Modern boards want matched pairs, because two
  sticks in the right slots run in **dual-channel** and roughly double memory bandwidth.
- **Speed** in MT/s (DDR4-3200, DDR5-5600). Faster is better only if the motherboard and
  CPU support it; otherwise the system downclocks, which is normal, not a fault.
- **Form factor.** **DIMM** for desktops, **SO-DIMM** for laptops and small-form-factor
  machines. They are not interchangeable, which is the point of the distinction.
- **Generation.** DDR4 and DDR5 have different notch positions and will not seat in each
  other's slots. Never mix generations; the board decides which generation is legal.

The failure modes and their symptoms: a module that is not fully seated produces a
no-boot or a beep code; a failing module produces random errors, application crashes and
**bit errors** that look like software problems. **ECC** memory detects and corrects
single-bit errors and is the answer for servers and workstations where a flipped bit is
unacceptable. **Parity** memory detects without correcting and is effectively historical.

## Storage devices

Know each type by its interface, its media and its trade:

- **Hard disk drive (HDD).** Magnetic spinning platters at 5400 or 7200 RPM. Cheap per
  terabyte, slow, mechanically fragile, and the part that is most likely to fail after
  five years of service.
- **Solid-state drive (SSD).** Flash memory, no moving parts, dramatically faster random
  access. SATA SSDs are limited by the SATA interface; **NVMe** drives over **M.2** use
  the PCIe bus and are several times faster again.
- **Removable and external.** USB flash drives, external drives, and **memory cards**
  (SD, microSD, CF) — the medium of cameras, phones and dash cams.
- **RAID**, covered in more depth in A5, is about the trade between speed, capacity and
  redundancy: **RAID 0** stripes for speed with no protection, **RAID 1** mirrors for
  redundancy, **RAID 5** stripes with parity for a compromise, **RAID 10** mirrors and
  stripes for performance with protection at the cost of half the capacity.

The **file systems** on those disks are separate from the hardware: **NTFS** for Windows,
**APFS** for modern macOS, **ext4** for most Linux systems, **exFAT** for removable media
that must work everywhere, and **FAT32** for compatibility with a 4 GB file-size ceiling
that still bites in the field.

## Motherboards, CPUs and add-on cards

The motherboard is the bus, the sockets and the firmware all at once. What the exam asks:

- **Form factor.** **ATX** full size, **microATX** and **mini-ITX** smaller — the choice
  constrains how many cards and drives fit.
- **CPU socket** must match the processor exactly; the socket type is the first
  compatibility check on any build.
- **Chipset** decides which features the board can offer — how many USB ports, whether
  overclocking is permitted, how many PCIe lanes are available.
- **Expansion slots.** **PCIe** x1, x4, x16 for graphics cards, network cards, storage
  controllers and capture cards. The physical slot length and the electrical lane count
  are separate facts, and a x16 card in a x4 slot works but runs slower.
- **Firmware.** **BIOS** is the classic firmware; **UEFI** is its modern replacement, with
  **Secure Boot** (refuses to run unsigned boot code), **GPT** partitioning for large
  disks, and a graphical setup screen. Firmware settings are where boot order, virtualization
  support and memory timing live.

**Cooling** belongs here too: a heat sink and fan on the CPU, case airflow front to back,
and thermal paste between the CPU and the heat sink. A machine that shuts down under load
is often a cooling problem before it is anything else.

## Power supplies

A power supply is specified by **wattage** (total capacity), **efficiency** (the 80 PLUS
ratings — Bronze, Gold, Platinum — describe how much input power becomes output), and
**modularity** (whether unused cables detach). Three practical rules:

1. Size the supply above the sum of the components' draw, with headroom for a graphics
   card and for aging.
2. Use the **correct connectors** for the card; never split a rail with adapters to make
   an underpowered supply fit.
3. A failing supply shows up as random shutdowns, restarts under load, or a machine that
   will not power on at all — and it is one of the few components that can take the rest
   of the machine with it.

## Multifunction devices and printer maintenance

A **multifunction device (MFD)** is a printer plus scanner plus copier plus fax, sharing
one paper path. The technologies to recognize:

- **Laser** — a drum, a laser that writes the image, toner, a fuser that melts the toner
  onto the paper. Fast per page, sharp text, and a fuser that gets very hot.
- **Inkjet** — liquid ink sprayed through a print head. Excellent photos, slower, and the
  print head clogs when unused.
- **Thermal** — heat applied to treated paper; receipt printers and label printers, no ink
  at all.
- **Impact/dot matrix** — a physical print head striking a ribbon; still used for
  multipart forms because it prints through carbon copies.

**Maintenance** is the practical half of the module. Clean the glass and the scanner
path; replace toner and drums on a laser, ink and print heads on an inkjet; clear jams by
opening the correct door rather than pulling paper backwards through the mechanism;
calibrate and align when output drifts; and know the consumables schedule so a printer
does not fail mid-quarter. A printer that is "slow" may simply be set to the wrong paper
type or running a draft mode off.

## Where this leads

Every part here returns in A5 as a fault: the display that flickers, the drive that
clicks, the supply that shuts the machine off under load, the printer that jams in the
same place every time. The virtualization module (A4) takes the hardware and asks what
happens when several operating systems share one physical machine.
