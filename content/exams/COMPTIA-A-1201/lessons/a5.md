# Hardware and Network Troubleshooting (A5)

*Module A5 — CompTIA A+ Core 1 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Every component in A3 and every connection in A2 eventually fails. This module is the
discipline for finding out which one, and it is deliberately the same loop the Network+
track teaches: identify, theorize, test, plan, verify, document. The hardware is specific;
the method is not.

## The method, applied to hardware

1. **Identify the problem.** What exactly happens? When did it start? What changed just
   before? Can you reproduce it? One fault at a time.
2. **Establish a theory.** Start with the obvious and the cheap — a loose cable, a full
   disk, a recent update. Consider several theories, then pick the most likely.
3. **Test the theory.** Confirm or discard it with the least invasive test available. A
   POST beep code costs nothing; swapping a motherboard does not.
4. **Plan the fix.** Know what the change affects and what you will do if it makes things
   worse. Back up data before any repair that touches storage.
5. **Verify.** Not "it powers on" — the user's actual workflow works, and the fault does
   not return under load.
6. **Document.** What was wrong, what you changed, and what you would check next time.

Two habits make the loop fast: **change one thing at a time**, so you know what fixed it;
and **test before you swap**, because a part you replaced on a hunch is money spent on a
guess.

## Motherboards, CPUs, RAM and power

The symptoms cluster, and the clusters are the lesson:

- **No power at all** (no fans, no lights): power supply, power cord, wall outlet, or the
  front-panel power switch connector. Test the supply before condemning the board.
- **Powers on, no display, no beep, fans spin:** CPU seating, RAM seating, or a failed
  board. Reseat memory and the CPU before replacing anything.
- **Beep codes and POST codes:** the board's own diagnostic language. Look up the code for
  the specific board or BIOS vendor; a repeating pattern of beeps usually means memory.
- **Random shutdowns or restarts under load:** overheating (dust, failed fan, dried
  thermal paste) or a failing power supply. Check temperatures first.
- **Random crashes, application errors, corrupted files:** failing RAM or a failing
  storage device. Run a memory test and check the drive's health before blaming software.
- **Swollen capacitors, burn marks, a burning smell:** the board is done. Stop powering it.

## Drives and RAID

- **Clicking or grinding** from a hard drive means mechanical failure. Stop using it and
  image it if the data matters; every further spin is a risk.
- **A drive that disappears and reappears** is usually a cable, a connector, or a
  controller — reseat and replace the cable before replacing the drive.
- **SMART warnings** are the drive's own early-warning system. Treat them as a schedule,
  not a suggestion: replace before failure.
- **RAID faults** are read from the controller, not the operating system. A degraded array
  (one member failed) still serves data and is the moment to replace the member; a failed
  array is a restore, not a repair. **RAID is not backup** — it protects against one disk
  failing, not against deletion, corruption, ransomware, or a controller failure.
- **A drive that is full** explains a surprising share of "slow" and "won't save"
  tickets, on desktops and phones alike.

## Video, projectors and displays

- **No image, but the machine is running:** check the input source on the monitor, the
  cable at both ends, and whether the display is plugged into the graphics card rather
  than the motherboard's video output on a desktop with a discrete card.
- **Flickering, colored lines, intermittent dropouts:** cable or connector first, then the
  display, then the GPU. A cable that works at 1080p and fails at 4K is a bandwidth
  problem, not a broken monitor.
- **Dim projector image:** lamp or laser hours, dust on the lens, ambient light, and
  distance. A projector that shuts down after a few minutes is usually a thermal or filter
  problem.
- **Wrong resolution or overscan:** the display settings, not the hardware. Refresh-rate
  mismatches produce the "it looks like it is vibrating" complaint.

## Mobile device issues

The faults specific to phones and tablets:

- **Battery** — short life (capacity loss), swelling (safety issue, replace now), or a
  device that will not charge (port lint, cable, charger, or a failed charging circuit).
- **Overheating** — a runaway app, a failing battery, charging under a pillow, or a case
  that traps heat. A device that is hot while idle is usually software.
- **Screen** — cracked glass, dead touch zones, discoloration, or a digitizer that is not
  aligned with the display.
- **Connectivity** — no signal, no Wi-Fi, Bluetooth that will not pair. Check the obvious
  settings and airplane mode before suspecting hardware; a case with metal in it can
  attenuate signal.
- **Liquid damage** — corrosion continues after the device dries. Power it off, do not
  charge it, and treat intermittent faults after a spill as liquid damage until proven
  otherwise.
- **The universal mobile fix** is a restart, then an update, then a factory reset after a
  backup. If all three fail, it is hardware.

## Network issues

- **No connectivity on one machine, others fine:** that machine's cable, NIC, or
  configuration. **No connectivity for everyone:** the switch, the router, or the ISP.
- **Link light off:** physical layer — cable, port, or NIC. **Link light on, no traffic:**
  configuration or a blocked port.
- **Intermittent wireless:** channel congestion, distance, interference, or a client that
  is sticky to a weak access point.
- **Slow network:** measure first. Latency, packet loss and jitter distinguish a congested
  link from a failing one, and a duplex mismatch from a saturated one.
- **Duplicate IP address** warnings mean two devices were given the same static address, or
  a static address sits inside the DHCP pool. The fix is the address plan, not the cable.

## Printer issues

- **Jams** — the paper type, a worn roller, or debris in the path. Pull paper in the
  direction of travel and check the door the manufacturer names.
- **Streaks and smears** — laser: dirty or worn drum, low toner, bad fuser. Inkjet: clogged
  print head, low ink, or a misaligned head.
- **Blank pages** — a protective tape still on a new cartridge, an empty cartridge, or the
  wrong driver.
- **Nothing prints but the queue fills** — spooler, driver, or connectivity; a print job
  stuck in the queue blocks everything behind it.
- **"Printer offline"** — the printer's network settings, a changed IP address, or the
  print queue pointing at the old one. Static addressing or a reservation on the DHCP
  server is the durable fix.
