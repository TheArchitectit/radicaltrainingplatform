# Mobile Devices and Accessories (A1)

*Module A1 — CompTIA A+ Core 1 study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

Phones and tablets are most of what a beginning technician actually touches. This lesson
covers the hardware you monitor and replace on a mobile device, the accessories and
connections that attach to it, and the network configuration and provisioning decisions
you make when a phone joins a company.

## Monitoring mobile device hardware

A phone is a small computer with a fixed parts list, and the failure rates are not evenly
distributed across it:

- **Battery** — the consumable. Lithium-ion cells lose capacity with every charge cycle;
  a two-year-old phone that dies at 30% or swells enough to lift its back cover needs a
  replacement, and a **swollen** battery is a safety item, not a convenience one — it gets
  replaced promptly and disposed of properly, never punctured.
- **Screen assembly** — glass, digitizer (the touch layer) and LCD or OLED behind it.
  Cracked glass with working touch is a glass replacement; dead touch zones or bleeding
  pixels mean the assembly. Most phone repairs replace all three as one unit.
- **Charging port** — the second most common failure after the battery, and often not a
  failure at all: pocket lint packed into the port prevents the cable from seating. Compressed
  air or a wooden pick fixes more "broken ports" than new parts do.
- **Cameras, speakers, microphones, haptics** — modular and replaceable on most models.

The technician's first tool here is the built-in diagnostics: most platforms have a
hardware test mode (dialer codes on phones, vendor diagnostics on tablets and laptops)
that exercises each component and reports pass or fail — run it before ordering parts.

## Accessories and connectivity options

The connector standards are the exam's favorite detail:

- **USB-C** — the modern universal connector: reversible, carries data, video, and power
  up to substantial wattages. USB 3.x/4 and Thunderbolt ride the same plug. Not every
  USB-C port does everything; the small icons beside the port (SS, lightning bolt) tell
  you which features are live.
- **Lightning** — Apple's older proprietary phone connector.
- **Micro-USB** — the trapezoid predecessor still found on cheap accessories; being
  forced to keep this cable alive is a normal part of the job.

Wireless attachment options each trade something:

- **Bluetooth** — about 10 meters, low bandwidth, pairs with explicit approval. Headsets,
  keyboards, watches.
- **Wi-Fi** — building scale, high bandwidth, the normal data path.
- **NFC** — a few centimeters, almost no bandwidth. Tap-to-pair and tap-to-pay; the short
  range **is** the security.
- **Cellular** — everywhere, bandwidth depends on plan and signal. The fallback when no
  Wi-Fi exists, and the reason a phone is useful outside the office.
- **USB tethering** — cable length, high bandwidth, shares the phone's data with one
  device and charges it while doing it.

Two concepts recur. **Hotspot/tethering** means the phone becomes the modem for other
devices — over Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or USB. And **wireless charging** uses inductive coils:
convenient, slower, and it heats the battery slightly, which is worth knowing when a
customer complains their phone gets warm overnight.

Synchronization deserves one honest paragraph: phones sync contacts, calendars, e-mail and
files with cloud services and with a host computer. The A+ answer for company data on a
personal phone is **containerization** — the company's e-mail and files live in a managed,
encrypted container the employer can wipe remotely, while personal photos stay personal
and untouched. That single design resolves most of the "bring your own device" arguments
you will hear in the field.

## Network connectivity and provisioning for mobile

A phone gets network settings the same way a laptop does — DHCP hands out address, mask,
gateway and DNS (see module A2) — with two mobile-specific twists:

- **Static addressing on a phone** exists but is rare (kiosk devices in a fixed
  environment); almost everything is DHCP or a cellular carrier's dynamic assignment.
- **E-mail settings** are usually installed by a **configuration profile** rather than
  typed: on iOS/Android this is the Exchange/ActiveSync or gateway account, with server,
  port, encryption (TLS) and credential fields that must match the mail server exactly.

**Provisioning** is the answer to "how do we get 40 phones set up the same way". Typing
accounts, Wi-Fi and policies 40 times guarantees 40 subtly different phones, so a
**MDM (mobile device management)** system handles it:

1. The device enrolls (scanning a QR code or tapping NFC once).
2. MDM installs configuration profiles: Wi-Fi, e-mail, certificates, VPN.
3. MDM enforces policy: passcode length and complexity, encryption on, camera or
   app-store restrictions, and **remote wipe** capability.
4. When the phone is lost, it is located or wiped — a wipe can be **full** (the device
   returns to factory state) or **selective** (only the managed container, which is the
   one you use on a personal device).

The policies that show up most in questions: passcode requirements, encryption (modern
phones ship encrypted by default — the policy question is whether you **require** it),
app allow/block lists, jailbreak/root detection (a rooted phone bypasses MDM trust and
should be treated as non-compliant), and geofencing — taking an action when a device
leaves a defined area, like an employee badge or a store device wandering off site.
