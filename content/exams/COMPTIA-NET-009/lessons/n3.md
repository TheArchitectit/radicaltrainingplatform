# Network Operations (N3)

*Module N3 — CompTIA Network+ study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

A built network is not a finished network. This lesson is about the part of the job that
never ends: the processes that keep change from becoming chaos, the monitoring that tells
you something is wrong before users do, the recovery plan you hope you never open, the
everyday services that make addressing and naming work, and the ways you reach and manage
the devices themselves.

## Processes and procedures

Three documents and three habits carry most of the weight at this level.

**The network documentation set.** A network diagram (physical: what plugs into what;
logical: how it addresses and routes), an **IP address scheme** showing what ranges are
where, a **port layout** or **rack diagram** for each closet, and a list of **standard
configurations** — the baseline every switch of a given kind should match. If a device
drifts from its standard configuration and nobody wrote down the standard, the drift looks
like a fault.

**Change management.** Nobody changes a production network on a hunch. A change gets
requested, reviewed for impact, signed off, scheduled into a **maintenance window**,
documented, and reviewed afterward. Three terms belong here:

- **Standard change** — pre-cleared, low-risk, repeatable (replacing a failed cable of a
  known type). Takes the fast path.
- **Normal change** — needs the review and approval it has not had before.
- **Emergency change** — happens now, gets reviewed afterwards. The post-review is not
  optional; it is what stops "emergency" from becoming a permanent excuse.

Related vocabulary you will see in scenario questions: **push** configuration to many
devices from a central system; **zero-touch** provisioning, where a new device configures
itself from a template when plugged in; and **firmware**, the software inside the device
itself, which you update deliberately, with a tested version and a rollback plan — never
because a banner offered an update.

**The habits:** time synchronization (see below), backups of device configurations, and
labeling. Cables and ports that are labeled correctly save the next person an hour;
labeled badly, they cost the next outage an hour.

## Monitoring: knowing before they call

A monitoring system answers two different questions, and tools tend to specialize:

- **Is it up?** **Up/down monitoring** and reachability checks. Cheap, and catches hard
  failures only.
- **How is it doing?** **Performance monitoring** — interface utilization, errors,
  discards, latency, packet loss, jitter. This is what makes "the network is slow"
  testable, because you compare against a **baseline** recorded when things were normal.
  Without a baseline there is no such thing as "slow", only opinion.

The mechanics: a poller asks devices for counters on a schedule; **SNMP** is the classic
protocol for reading those counters, and a **trap** or an **SNMP inform** is how a device
pushes an alert instead of waiting to be asked. Newer setups stream or pull data from the
device programmatically rather than polling — and the exam cares about the distinction
between **pull** (I ask, on my schedule) and **push** (you tell me, when it happens).

**Syslog** deserves its own line: devices send timestamped event messages to a central
collector, so that when three switches all flap at 02:14 you can see it in one place rather
than on three screens. Timestamps only mean something if the devices agree on the time,
which is why **NTP** and a time source are part of monitoring, not an afterthought.

Where the data comes from matters too: an **agent** installed on a host reports what
happens inside it, while a **flow** record (NetFlow-style) tells you who talked to whom,
how much, and when, from the network's point of view. Agent data explains a slow server;
flow data explains unexpected traffic.

**SNMP versions** in one line: v1 and v2c authenticate with a community string sent in
clear text and encrypt nothing; v3 adds authentication and encryption. If a question
mentions capturing credentials off the wire, that is the difference it is testing.

## Disaster recovery

A **backup** copies data. A **disaster recovery plan** says how the business runs when the
site it lives in does not, and it is written before you need it. Three numbers define it:

- **RPO — recovery point objective.** How much data you are willing to lose, measured in
  time. An RPO of four hours means the newest acceptable copy is four hours old.
- **RTO — recovery time objective.** How long you are willing to be down while recovering.
- **MTBF / MTTR** describe the hardware underneath: mean time between failures, and mean
  time to repair. A short RTO on components that take days to replace is a plan that
  depends on spare parts being on the shelf.

The three classic recovery sites, ordered by cost and readiness:

- **Cold site** — space, power and connectivity, no equipment ready. Cheapest, slowest.
- **Warm site** — some equipment, not current. The middle ground.
- **Hot site** — a full mirrored environment able to take over quickly. Most expensive,
  least downtime.

Backup **where** is the other half. **On-site** restores fastest and dies with the server
room. **Off-site** survives a building loss. **Offline** (powered down, disconnected,
air-gapped) is the specific answer to ransomware, because an attacker who has the network
cannot encrypt a copy that is not on it. The pattern behind those three is the familiar
**3-2-1**: several copies, on different media, one of them somewhere else entirely.

What you back up has a name worth knowing: **full, incremental, differential**. A full
backup copies everything; an incremental copies only what changed since the last backup of
any kind, which is quick to take and slow to restore; a differential copies everything
changed since the last *full*, which is slower to take and much simpler to restore. Which
one is "best" depends entirely on whether restore speed or backup window is your RTO
problem. And a backup nobody has rehearsed restoring is a hope, not a backup — the plan is
only proven when someone has actually restored from it.

## IPv4 and IPv6 services

Two services do most of the work, and both already appeared in A+ networking:

- **DHCP** hands out addresses and options. The four settings every host needs — address,
  mask, gateway, DNS — are exactly what a DHCP lease carries, plus a lease time. A
  **scope** is the pool a DHCP server serves; a **reservation** pins one known device (by
  its MAC) to one address so it is predictable without being hand-configured; an
  **exclusion** keeps a range out of the pool for devices that must be static.
  IPv6 uses its own mechanisms — stateless address autoconfiguration lets a host build its
  own address from the router's advertisement, and stateless does not track leases;
  stateful (DHCPv6) does, and is what you use when you also need to hand out other
  settings.
- **DNS** resolves names. The records to recognize: **A** (name to IPv4), **AAAA** (name
  to IPv6), **CNAME** (an alias for another name), **MX** (where mail for this domain
  goes), **TXT** (free-form text, used for verification and mail policy), **PTR**
  (address back to name, the reverse direction), **NS** (which servers are authoritative
  for the zone), and **SRV** (which host provides a service, on which port).

Then the small services that are easy to forget until they fail: **NTP** for time, and a
**TFTP** server for devices to fetch configuration and firmware — a device with no
credentials to give will happily pull a file over TFTP, which is exactly why it is a
concern.

## Access and management methods

Reachability and manageability are different properties, and the exam asks about both:

- **Out-of-band management** — a dedicated management port or console connection that does
  not depend on the production network carrying your traffic. When the network is down,
  in-band management is down with it. This is the reason the console cable is in the bag.
- **In-band** — managing a device over the same network it is part of. Convenient, and
  unavailable precisely when you most need it.
- **Remote access** to the device itself: **SSH** for a command line (encrypted), **HTTPS**
  for a web interface (encrypted), **console/serial** for first-time setup or a broken
  device, and **RDP** when the target is a Windows host rather than a network device.
  **Telnet** and plain HTTP send credentials visibly and should be treated as the wrong
  answer unless a question specifically asks about them.
- **Network access control** decides what a connecting device is allowed to touch: 802.1X
  authenticates before granting port access, a **guest network** or captive portal
  separates visitors from staff, and MAC or certificate-based checks say which devices may
  join at all.
