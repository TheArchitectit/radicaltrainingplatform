# Network Implementation (N2)

*Module N2 — CompTIA Network+ study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

The concepts lesson (N1) explained what travels across a network. This lesson is about the
gear that moves it: how a router decides where a packet goes, how a switch keeps local
traffic local, how to pick a wireless setup that fits a real building, and what has to be
true in the wiring closet for any of it to work.

## Routing: how a router chooses

A router has one job — forward packets between networks — and it does it by looking up the
destination IP address in a **routing table**. Each entry says, in effect: "for this
network, send the packet to that next hop, out this interface." A default route
(`0.0.0.0/0` for IPv4) catches everything the table does not match explicitly, which is
why a small office can reach the whole internet with one route.

Routes get into the table three ways:

- **Static** — an administrator types them in. Predictable, no protocol traffic, and
  exactly as current as the person who last edited it. Best for small, stable networks
  and for the default route pointing at an ISP.
- **Dynamic** — routers exchange routing updates and learn from each other. When a link
  fails, the protocol recalculates. Best when the topology changes or is too large to
  hand-maintain.
- **Directly connected** — any interface that is up and configured puts its own network
  in the table automatically. Free, and the reason a router can talk to the networks it
  is plugged into.

Two protocol families show up on this exam, and the distinction is the point:

- **Distance vector** protocols (RIP being the classic) choose a path by hop count and
  tell their neighbors the whole picture they know. Simple to configure, slow to
  converge, and prone to routing loops without safeguards.
- **Link state** protocols (OSPF being the one to know) each router builds a full map of
  the area, then computes shortest paths itself. Faster convergence, more CPU and memory,
  far better in a real enterprise.

A useful shorthand for **administrative distance**: when two protocols both know a route
to the same network, the router believes the source with the lower number. Static routes
beat dynamic ones by default for exactly this reason. And inside one protocol, the
**metric** decides which of two equally believable paths wins.

## Switching: keeping local traffic local

A switch learns on its own. It looks at the **source MAC address** of every frame and
records which port it arrived on, then forwards future frames only to the port where the
destination MAC lives. That is unicast, and it is why a switch is not a hub — a hub
repeats everything everywhere, a switch does not. Until a switch knows a destination, it
floods the frame to every port in the broadcast domain, so a MAC table full of guesses is
a sign of a network that is not talking normally.

Three switching features matter at this level:

- **VLANs** split one physical switch into several logical broadcast domains. Ports are
  assigned to a VLAN; hosts in different VLANs cannot hear each other's broadcasts and
  need a router (or a layer-3 switch) to communicate. The payoff is containment — a
  misbehaving host, or a guest network, stays inside its own VLAN.
- **Trunk ports** carry several VLANs over a single cable between switches, tagging each
  frame so the far end knows which VLAN it belongs to. An **access port** belongs to
  exactly one VLAN and carries untagged frames, which is what you connect a workstation
  to. Mixing these two up is one of the most common beginner configuration faults.
- **Spanning tree** exists because redundancy is dangerous. Two switches joined by two
  cables loop frames forever, and a broadcast storm follows in seconds. Spanning tree
  blocks one path logically so the loop cannot carry traffic, then unblocks it if the
  active path dies. The trade you accept is a blocked port that looks idle but is
  insurance.

## Wireless: choosing devices and technology

Three knobs decide how a wireless network behaves:

- **Band.** 2.4 GHz reaches farther and gets through walls, and is crowded — microwave
  ovens, Bluetooth and every neighbor's network live there. 5 GHz is faster and cleaner
  but shorter ranged. 6 GHz adds wide clean channels in the newest standard, at the
  shortest range of the three.
- **Channel.** Adjacent networks on the same channel fight for airtime. In 2.4 GHz, only
  channels 1, 6 and 11 do not overlap each other, so a site survey that places three
  access points on 1, 6 and 11 beats four access points all on 6.
- **Standards.** 802.11ac is 5 GHz only. 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) works on both bands and is
  built for many clients in the same room, which is what a classroom or an office
  actually is. A client can only use what both it and the access point support, so one
  old laptop caps the whole conversation.

Placement is physics, not taste: an access point wants to be central, high, and clear of
metal and dense walls. Coverage planning means putting enough access points, on
non-overlapping channels, that each client has one strong signal to prefer.

## Physical installation

Everything above depends on cabling and power being right, and this is where careless work
comes back later as "intermittent" problems:

- **Cable types.** Twisted-pair copper (Cat 5e and up) carries Ethernet over short runs.
  **Single-mode fiber** uses a laser and goes kilometers — the link between buildings.
  **Multimode fiber** uses a cheaper light source and is for shorter runs inside a
  building. Fiber is immune to electrical interference; copper is not, so it stays away
  from fluorescent lighting and power runs, and where it must cross power it crosses at a
  right angle rather than running alongside.
- **Standards and distances.** Structured cabling puts a wall jack near every workstation
  and runs it back to a patch panel in a closet. The practical limit to remember for
  twisted-pair Ethernet is 100 meters from the patch panel to the device, patch cords
  included.
- **PoE** delivers power over the same cable as data, which is how access points, cameras
  and IP phones get installed in ceilings without an electrician. The switch port supplies
  it; the classes determine what a device may draw.
- **Racks, UPS and environmental basics.** Equipment gets mounted in racks with airflow
  planned front-to-back, and a UPS keeps a power blip from becoming an outage — with
  conditioning for brownouts, which a plain power strip does not handle.
