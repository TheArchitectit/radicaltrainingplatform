# Network Security (N4)

*Module N4 — CompTIA Network+ study track. Independent study material — see the disclaimer at the end.*

N1 through N3 built a network that works and keeps working. This lesson is about the part
that keeps it working *for the right people*. It covers why network security is designed
the way it is, the attacks a network actually sees and what each one damages, and the
defenses that sit between those attacks and your traffic.

## Why network security looks the way it does

Three ideas explain almost every control in this module.

**Defense in depth.** No single control is trusted to hold. A packet crosses several
checkpoints — wireless authentication, a port access control, a firewall rule, an
intrusion system — so that when one is wrong or bypassed, the next one still applies. A
design with one very strong wall and nothing behind it is a design with an outage waiting
to happen.

**Least privilege and implicit deny.** Devices and users get the access they need and not
a field more. Firewalls embody this by default: a **deny-all** inbound posture with
narrowly written exceptions beats an allow-all list with narrow exceptions, because the
deny-all fails safe. When you cannot explain what a rule exists to permit, that rule is
risk you are carrying without knowing it.

**Confidentiality, integrity, availability.** Every attack in the next section damages at
least one of the three, and every control below protects at least one. Saying which is the
fastest way to reason about a security question: ransomware attacks availability *and*
confidentiality; a tampered firmware update attacks integrity; a cable cut attacks
availability only.

A fourth habit belongs here too: **segmentation**. A flat network means one foothold means
everything. VLANs and firewalls split the network so that the guest Wi-Fi cannot reach the
file server, and a printer cannot talk to a domain controller. The word to recognize is
that a compromise should stay **contained** — the same reason we did not run the whole
company off the building's HVAC controller, if it had been networked.

## Attacks, by what they do

Group the attacks by their effect and they stop being a list to memorize.

**Overwhelming availability (denial of service).** A **DoS** floods a service with more
traffic than it can serve; a **DDoS** does it from many compromised hosts at once, which
is why blocking one source does not help. **Amplification** makes it worse: the attacker
sends a small request with your victim's address as the reply-to, and an open resolver
answers many times larger to the victim. The defense at this level is mostly upstream —
rate limiting, and a provider that scrubs traffic before it reaches you.

**Listening and imitating.** On a shared medium, a host set to **promiscuous mode** reads
traffic not addressed to it. **ARP** poisoning tells a host "the router is at my MAC
address," so its traffic comes to you instead; **DNS** spoofing answers name lookups with
an address you control; **MAC flooding** overruns a switch's address table until it
degrades into a hub, repeating everything. All three are the same move — *become the middle
of the path* — and the umbrella term you will see is a **man-in-the-middle**. Rogue access
points and **evil twin** networks do the same thing at layer 1 and 2: broadcast the SSID
your users already trust, then capture what joins. The user-facing defense is boring and
real: notice when a familiar site suddenly wants a password you already used this morning.

**Attacking authentication directly.** **Brute force** tries passwords until one works.
**Credential stuffing** tries username and password pairs bought from a **different** site's
leak — which works only because people reuse them. **Password spraying** is its quieter
inverted version: one common password tried against many accounts, deliberately staying
under a per-account lockout threshold. **Pass-the-hash** reuses captured credentials
without ever cracking them. Countermeasures: account **lockout** thresholds, MFA,
password length over complexity theater, and unique credentials per system.

**Abusing trust and input.** **IP spoofing** forges a source address so traffic appears to
come from somewhere trusted — which is why a perimeter device that filters traffic should
drop inbound packets claiming to be from inside your own network, and why the answer is
called **anti-spoofing**. **Pointer redirection** and **buffer overflow** both exploit a
program trusting input about how much data is coming. **XML injection** and other
injection attacks hand a program's interpreter instructions you meant as data.

**Automated reconnaissance and exploitation.** **Privilege escalation** turns any foothold
into a better one. **Pass-the-ticket**, **driver malware** and **persistent shell**
maintain the foothold. **Zero-day** means a flaw with no fix available yet, which is why
patching alone cannot be the whole plan.

**Attacks on people.** **Phishing** is email; **smishing** is SMS; **vishing** is a phone
call; **spoofing** is impersonating a known number or address; **tailgating** is walking
through a door behind someone who belongs there; **shoulder surfing** is reading a screen
over a shoulder; **pretexting** is inventing a plausible story to justify asking;
**baiting** is leaving something tempting where curiosity does the rest; **watering hole**
means compromising a site a target group already visits; **typosquatting** is a domain
misspelled by one character; **brand impersonation** is a page that looks like a vendor's
login. None of these require a vulnerability in your software — the vulnerability is
helpfulness. The technical controls that help are mail filtering, MFA so a stolen password
is not enough, and training that tells people it is fine to report being fooled.

## Defenses: what goes where

**Firewalls and rules.** A firewall decides which flows are allowed. Know the axis each
rule filters on: source and destination address, port and protocol, application, and
**URL or category** (a web filter). A rule's position matters as much as its content,
because most firewalls evaluate top-down and **stop at the first match** — a broad
allow-listed rule above a narrow deny makes the deny unreachable. The three placement
patterns to recognize: a **screened subnet** or **DMZ** puts public-facing servers in a
neutral zone reachable from outside but not trusted inside; a **bastion** or jump host is
the single sanctioned way in to a protected network; and **network address translation**
incidentally hides internal addressing behind one public address.

**Detection and prevention.** An **IDS** watches and alerts. An **IPS** sits in the path
and blocks. In **signature mode** both match known attack patterns — reliable, and blind to
anything new. In **anomaly mode** they compare against a baseline and flag deviations —
catches new things, and cries wolf on a Tuesday when the baseline was built on a Monday.
A **honeypot** is a decoy worth nothing on purpose: activity on it is always suspicious,
because nothing legitimate was told to go there.

**Access control on the network itself.** **802.1X** with a **RADIUS** or **TACACS+**
server authenticates a device before the port carries its traffic. RADIUS carries
authentication and authorization for network access; TACACS+ separates authentication,
authorization and accounting and encrypts the whole session, which is why it tends to be
the answer for **device administration** specifically. **NAC** checks posture before letting
a device in. A **guest network** or **captive portal** serves visitors. **Port security**
limits which MAC addresses a switch port will learn, which is the direct answer to both
MAC flooding and someone unplugging a phone and plugging in a laptop.

**Wireless defenses.** WPA2 and WPA3 with a strong passphrase; **WPA3-Enterprise** and
802.1X instead of a shared key when there are users to authenticate individually, because
a shared key means everyone shares one secret and one former employee. Management frame
protection stops the deauthentication floods that knock clients off a network. And
**disable WPS** — the pin-based setup it offers is the weakest door on the device.

**Segmentation and secure tunnels.** **VLANs** are the layer-2 answer; **ACLs** are the
layer-3 answer; a **private VLAN** keeps hosts in one subnet from talking to each other at
all. For traffic across networks you do not own, a **VPN** builds a tunnel. Site-to-site
connects two offices; remote access connects one worker. Know the protocols by shape:
IPsec protects at the IP layer and is common for site-to-site; TLS-based VPNs (OpenVPN
style) ride on TCP/443 and get through most firewalls that block everything else.

**Hardening and hygiene.** Change default credentials and disable unused services. Patch on
a schedule, and patch **firmware** on network gear — it is a computer, and it is frequently
the part nobody patches. Keep a **baseline configuration** for each device type, and
diff a device against it to find drift. Use encrypted management only — SSH, HTTPS — never
Telnet or HTTP. Maintain an **asset inventory**: you cannot defend what you do not know
exists.

**Physical and residual.** **Faraday bags** block radio on a seized or lost device. A
**cable lock** and a locked closet are still the only real control for many laptops. And
**data destruction** — purge, degauss, shred — is what makes a decommissioned drive safe to
release; deleting a file and formatting a volume are neither.

**Risk vocabulary, briefly.** A **threat** is the bad thing that could happen, a
**vulnerability** is the weakness that lets it, and a **risk** is the chance of the two
meeting times the damage done. **Mitigate** it (add a control), **transfer** it (buy
insurance or outsource it), **avoid** it (stop doing the activity), or **accept** it in
writing. Transfers and acceptances are business decisions, which is why the exam sometimes
asks for the option that costs a control entirely.

## Where this leads

N5 brings concepts, implementation and operations together under the troubleshooting
method — and a large share of the faults you meet will look like outages and turn out to
be security: a rule added on Friday, a certificate that expired, a port shut down by port
security because someone moved a desk.
